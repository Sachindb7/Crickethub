The Slow Death of ODI Cricket: Why 50-Over Matches Are Disappearing Forever

CricketHub June 27, 2026 6 min read

Remember when a 5-match bilateral ODI series was the highlight of the winter? You would rush home from school, switch on the TV, and watch Sachin and Sehwag open the innings. The middle overs were slow, the fielding was spread out, and a score of 260 was considered match-winning.

Today, 260 is chased down in 18 overs in the IPL. And the 50-over format is quietly suffocating in the corner, ignored by broadcasters, players, and fans alike.

The Middle-Over Void

The problem with modern ODI cricket is the middle overs. Between over 15 and 40, the game basically goes to sleep. In the past, this was a battle of skill — rotating the strike against spinners. But our attention spans have shrunk. T20 cricket has trained our brains to expect a boundary or a wicket every six balls. Watching two batters push singles for two hours feels like an eternity.

Test cricket survives because it offers unparalleled drama, narrative arcs, and severe testing of character. T20 survives because it is pure entertainment and adrenaline. ODI cricket is caught in no man's land — it is too long to be pure entertainment, and not pure enough to be the ultimate test of skill.

The Franchise Factor

Players are also walking away. Why would a fast bowler put his body through 10 overs of bowling and 50 overs of fielding in an empty stadium for an international bilateral series, when he could bowl 4 overs in a packed stadium for a franchise league and earn ten times the money?

The 50-over World Cup is the only thing keeping the format alive. Once every four years, we pretend to care about ODIs. But the writing is on the wall. Bilateral ODI series are already shrinking to 3 matches. Soon, they might disappear entirely, leaving behind a 40-year legacy of colored clothing, white balls, and memories of a slower, simpler era of cricket.

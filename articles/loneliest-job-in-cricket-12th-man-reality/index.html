The Loneliest Job in Cricket: What It Actually Feels Like to Be the 12th Man

The Illusion of Being There

You are in the Indian cricket team. You wear the same blue jersey. You stay in the same five-star hotel. You travel on the same team bus. Fans ask for your autograph outside the stadium. On paper, you have achieved the dream that millions of kids play gully cricket for.

But then the match starts, eleven men walk out to the middle, and you sit in the dugout. You are the 12th man. And suddenly, you realize that being in the team and being of the team are two very different things.

The Drinks Break Psychology

There is a specific humiliation in being one of the best cricketers in the country and your primary job being the delivery of energy drinks.

When the umpire calls for a drinks break, the 12th man sprints onto the field carrying a fluorescent rack of bottles. You hand them out to players who are sweating, swearing, and engaged in a fierce battle. You offer a quick towel, maybe a piece of advice you noticed from the boundary line, but mostly, you just listen.

The players on the field are speaking a language you can't participate in. They are talking about how the pitch is gripping, how the wind is swirling from the pavilion end, how the fast bowler's release point has dropped. You nod, but you don't really know. Because you're not in the fight. You are a spectator with better seating and a team logo on your shirt.

Did You Know?

In modern cricket, the 12th man actually has specific tactical duties. They often carry messages from the coach, observe field placements from different angles, and track the condition of the ball. It's not just about water anymore, but the psychological toll remains the same.

The Guilt of Hoping for an Injury

This is the darkest secret of the reserve bench: To get your chance, someone else has to fail, or someone else has to get hurt.

Nobody wants their teammate to break a finger or tear a hamstring. These are your friends. But deep down, in the quiet corners of your mind when you're watching the opening batter score his third consecutive century, a tiny voice whispers: "If he keeps playing like this, I will never get a game."

It's a psychological torture chamber. You have to actively cheer for the success of the person who is keeping you out of the team. You have to applaud when they hit a boundary, knowing that every run they score pushes your debut further into the distance. It requires a level of mental fortitude that coaches rarely talk about.

The Unseen Labor

When the match ends, the playing eleven goes back to the hotel for ice baths and massages. The 12th man? He heads to the practice nets.

Because you haven't played, you have to simulate match intensity. You face throwdowns for an hour in an empty stadium while the groundsmen clean up the garbage in the stands. You run sprints to keep your fitness levels up. You work twice as hard to stay ready for an opportunity that might never come.

And when the tour is over, the stats will say you played 0 matches, scored 0 runs, and took 0 wickets. But only you know how heavy those bottles felt.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a 12th man do in cricket?

A 12th man is a substitute fielder who can replace a player on the field in case of injury. They also carry drinks, equipment, and tactical messages from the coach to the players on the field.

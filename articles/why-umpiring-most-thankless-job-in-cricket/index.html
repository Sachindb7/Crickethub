Behind the Scenes

Why Umpiring is the Most Thankless Job in Cricket

CricketHub June 26, 2026 5 min read

If a batter makes a mistake, he gets out. The crowd sighs, the commentators analyze the false shot, and the game moves on. If a bowler bowls a wide full toss, he gets hit for a six. The captain shakes his head, sets the field again, and the game moves on.

If an umpire makes a mistake, the entire stadium erupts. Social media demands their firing. Former players write columns questioning their competence. The match gets paused for minutes while slow-motion replays, ball-tracking algorithms, and Snickometers dissect their human error in super high-definition.

The Terror of Technology

Before DRS (Decision Review System), the umpire's word was law. Today, the umpire is just a highly stressed middleman waiting to be corrected by a computer.

Imagine doing your job while 50,000 people scream at you, knowing that if you make a miscalculation by literally two millimeters, a giant screen will immediately broadcast your failure to the world. A ball moving at 145kmph hits the pad. You have roughly 1.5 seconds to calculate the trajectory, the bounce, the deviation off the seam, and whether there was an inside edge. You say "Not Out."

Then comes the review. The giant screen shows the ball clipping the outside of the off-stump by a millimeter. The crowd boos. The commentators say "Oh, that's a poor decision."

The Unseen Perfection

What nobody talks about is that modern elite umpires operate at an accuracy rate of over 95%. Think about that. They get it right 95 times out of 100 with the naked eye. But nobody remembers the 95 brilliant decisions. They only remember the 5 times DRS overturned them.

Next time you see an umpire standing in the sun for 6 hours, dealing with angry fast bowlers and screaming crowds, remember: They are the only people on the field expected to be completely flawless, with zero reward for being perfect.
